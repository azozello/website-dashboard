# GA4 Collects Everything; Plausible Collects Enough

As of November 2026, Google Analytics 4 exposes **86 standard metrics and 374 standard dimensions** through its Data API (schema last updated 10 Nov 2026), plus roughly **80 metrics that exist only in its UI and Explorations**. Plausible Analytics exposes **14 metrics and 32 dimensions** through its Stats API v2, and **18 distinct metrics** across dashboard and API combined. The gap is smaller than the counts suggest: **53 of GA4's 86 API metrics and 195 of its 374 dimensions** serve ecommerce, Google's ad platforms, publisher ads or mobile apps. That leaves a web-traffic core of about 20 metrics, and Plausible covers most of it. What Plausible can't cover is anything that needs a persistent visitor identity, such as returning users, cohorts and lifetime value. The two products differ more in features and in how they collect data. GA4 is free and sets two-year cookies, so EU sites need a consent banner, and it models the visitors who decline only once a site has about 1,000 consenting users a day. Its 2025–2026 additions mostly serve advertisers. Plausible costs **$9–$19 a month at 10k pageviews** and runs cookieless on EU-owned infrastructure. It counts every non-bot visit but forgets each visitor after 24 hours, and it keeps custom properties, funnels, revenue and its API on the Business tier and above. For Rate 1, both incumbents leave the same three openings. Their Search Console views are shallow: GA4's has no time-series charts and joins only to landing page, device and country, and Plausible's shows only queries with at least one click, fetched live and never stored. Their form tracking counts submissions but captures no lead. And Plausible has no official Wix integration.

The report gives GA4's metrics and then its features, then Plausible's metrics and features in the same order, and ends with a side-by-side of the points that matter to Rate 1.

## GA4 metrics: four automatic events feed 86 API metrics and 374 dimensions

GA4 records every interaction as an event: a page view, a scroll and a purchase each arrive as a named event with parameters, and all metrics and dimensions are computed from those events. On a web stream, every event automatically carries `language`, `page_location`, `page_referrer`, `page_title` and `screen_resolution`. Parameter values are capped at **100 characters**, except page_title (300), page_referrer (420) and page_location (1,000) ([Google: automatically collected events](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9234069)). gtag.js also attaches `ga_session_id` (the session start timestamp) and `ga_session_number` to every event in a session. Measurement Protocol and Data Import events don't get them ([Google: sessions](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9191807)). An `is_engaged_session_event` flag on every event feeds engagement rate and bounce rate ([Google: engagement rate](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/12195621)).

### A default website gets 4 automatic events and 9 Enhanced Measurement events

With only the tag installed, a GA4 web stream records **`first_visit`, `session_start`, `user_engagement` and `page_view`**. Enhanced Measurement is toggled per web stream and needs no code. It adds outbound clicks, site search, YouTube video, file downloads, form interactions and a scroll event that fires **once, at 90% depth only** ([Google: Enhanced Measurement](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9216061)). Google also makes the site owner responsible for keeping personal data out of these events. Leads, logins, ecommerce and every other business event must be hand-tagged against Google's recommended-event schema. Most of the other "automatic" events exist only in Firebase app streams.

### 86 Data API metrics, of which roughly 20 describe web traffic

The Data API v1 schema lists **86 standard metrics in ten families** ([Google: Data API schema](https://developers.google.com/analytics/devguides/reporting/data/v1/api-schema)). Definitions matter when comparing products. An **engaged session** lasts longer than 10 seconds (adjustable per stream), contains a key event, or has two or more page views. Engagement rate is engaged sessions divided by sessions, and **bounce rate is its complement**. That is a different definition from Universal Analytics' single-hit bounce, so the two numbers aren't comparable ([Google: engagement rate](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/12195621)). Sessions time out after **30 minutes** by default, and the timeout is adjustable ([Google: sessions](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9191807)). GA4 counts users three ways. Total users is anyone who logged an event. Active users, the "Users" shown in reports, are those with an engaged session, a first_visit or recorded engagement time. New users are those with a first_visit ([Google: user metrics](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/12253918)). Reports estimate users and sessions with HyperLogLog++, usually within 1% of the exact counts in a BigQuery export ([Google: sampling](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/13331292)).

Of those, **195 belong to Campaign Manager 360, Display & Video 360, Search Ads 360 and Google Ads** ([Google: Data API schema](https://developers.google.com/analytics/devguides/reporting/data/v1/api-schema)). Small-business sites rarely need those fields. The 30 manual/UTM fields matter more: per scope, they cover source, medium, source/medium, campaign name and ID, term, content, source platform, creative format and marketing tactic.

Demographics come from **Google signals**, which uses data from users signed in to Google with ad personalization on and can be turned off per region ([Google: Google signals](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9445345)). They cover age brackets 18-24 through 65+, gender and affinity-style interests ([Google: dimensions and metrics](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9143382)). Age, gender and interest data is **always kept for only 2 months**, whatever the property's retention setting ([Google: data retention](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/7667196)). Three dimension changes landed in 2026 ([Google: what's new](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9164320)). On 13 May, an **"AI Assistant" default channel** started classifying referrals from ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and similar tools with medium `ai-assistant`. On 11 June, a **Source group** field began consolidating platform variants such as Facebook, Instagram, TikTok and Perplexity, applied retroactively. It is not yet in the Data API schema. Hostname exclude and include data filters arrived on 11 June and 21 September. The Search Console query dimensions can't be crossed with Analytics dimensions, and the landing-page report breaks down only by country and device ([Google: Search Console](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10737381)).

### Custom definitions and four layers that alter what reports show

Custom parameters aren't reportable until each one is registered as a custom dimension or metric. Registration isn't retroactive, and new definitions take 24–48 hours to appear. Values that look numeric are coerced to numbers ([Google: custom dimensions and metrics](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10075209)). GA4 360 raises every quota by 2–10×.

| Limit | Standard | GA4 360 |
|---|---|---|
| User / event / item-scoped custom dimensions | 25 / 50 / 10 | 100 / 125 / 25 |
| Custom metrics / calculated metrics | 50 / 5 | 125 / 50 |
| Parameters per event / user properties | 25 / 25 | 100 / 100 |
| Event parameter value length | 100 chars | 500 chars |
| Key events / audiences | 30 / 100 | 50 / 400 |
| Saved segments / comparisons | 50 / 50 | 200 / 200 |
| Explorations | 200 per user, 500 shared | 200 per user, 1,000 shared |
| Distinct event names | unlimited on web; 500 per app user | unlimited on web; 2,000 per app user |
| Data API quota | 200,000 tokens/day | 2M tokens/day |
| BigQuery daily export | 1M events/day | billions |
| Data import storage | 10 GB | 1 TB |

Sources: [custom dimensions and metrics](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10075209), [Analytics 360 limits](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11202874), [collection limits](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9267744). Further limits: user property names can be at most 24 characters and values at most 36. User-ID can be up to 256 characters.

GA4 can alter what a report shows in four ways. **Thresholding** withholds rows when demographics, audiences built on demographics, or low-volume search queries could identify people. It is system-defined and can't be switched off ([Google: thresholds](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9383630)). **Sampling** applies only in Explorations, above **10M events per query** on standard and up to 1B on 360. Unsampled explorations are 360-only ([Google: sampling](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/13331292)). The **"(other)" row** groups the least common values once a table exceeds its row limit. Google treats more than 500 values per dimension as high-cardinality ([Google: other row](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/13331684)). **Modeled data** comes in two forms. Consent-mode behavioral modeling estimates users and sessions, but not event counts, for visitors who decline cookies. It requires advanced consent mode, **at least 1,000 denied-consent events a day for 7 days, and at least 1,000 consenting daily users on 7 of the previous 28 days**. It appears only under the Blended reporting identity and never in BigQuery, audiences, user explorer, cohort, user-lifetime, retention or predictive views ([Google: behavioral modeling](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11161109)). The second form is modeled key events: Google estimates conversions it can't observe, revises attributed conversions for up to 12 days, and credits low-confidence ones to Direct ([Google: modeled key events](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10710245)).

### Two-year cookies, no stored EU IPs, and 2- or 14-month retention

GA4 identifies browsers with two first-party cookies, **`_ga` (client ID) and `_ga_<container-id>` (session state), each with a 2-year default expiry**. The expiry can be overridden to anything from immediate to 25 months. Browsers shorten cookie life for visitors who don't return (Chrome to 400 days, Safari to 7) ([Google: cookie usage](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11397207)). Sites can add a `user_id` of up to 256 characters for signed-in users. It must not contain personal data ([Google: User-ID](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9213390)). When analytics storage is denied through Consent Mode, no client ID is stored ([Google: data collection](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11593727)). Google states that GA4 **does not log or store IP addresses from EU, Swiss or UK users**. Their traffic is collected through EU, Swiss or UK servers, and the IP is used only to derive coarse location before being discarded. **Granular location and device collection** is on by default and can be switched off per region. Switching it off blanks city, browser and OS minor versions, the User-Agent string, device brand, model and name, and screen resolution, and it reduces modeled conversions ([Google: EU data and privacy](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/12017362)). Client-side **data redaction** removes email addresses and any query parameters the owner lists. It is on by default for new properties, covers web streams only, and works on a best-effort basis ([Google: data redaction](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/13544947)). User- and event-level data is kept for **2 or 14 months**, or up to 50 months on 360. Large properties are forced to 2 months. The setting limits only Explorations and funnel reports; aggregated standard reports persist beyond it ([Google: data retention](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/7667196)). Search Console data in GA4 is capped at 16 months, and Google Business Profile data is a rolling 6-month window.

Reports are assembled from a customizable library of collections ([Google: collections](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/12924488)). Most features added in 2025–2026 serve advertisers: conversion management, cross-channel budgeting, automatic import of Meta and TikTok costs, Customer Match and the Data Manager. A smaller set serves small businesses directly: lead reports, Google Business Profile reports, Dashboards and the AI Assistant channel. GA4's legal footing in the EU still depends on consent. The 2022 rulings by the Austrian, French and Italian data protection authorities found Google Analytics transfers unlawful ([Matomo GDPR timeline](https://matomo.org/gdpr-timeline/)). The EU General Court upheld the EU-US Data Privacy Framework in *Latombe* on **3 Sep 2025**, but an appeal is pending ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/european-general-court-dismisses-latombe-challenge-upholds-eu-us-data-privacy-framework)). Commentators agree that the Framework settles data transfers, not the ePrivacy cookie-consent requirement ([PrivyCDP](https://privycdp.org/regulation/google-analytics-gdpr-2026)). Small businesses' complaints cluster around the same points. Thresholding can't be disabled; one small WooCommerce store saw about 15% fewer conversions in GA4 than in its store data ([Funnel.io](https://help.funnel.io/en/articles/16746941-troubleshoot-missing-data-caused-by-thresholding-in-ga4)). Vendors selling alternatives put the losses at 30–60% from consent refusal and 10–20% from blockers, but those figures are unverified ([Clickport, vendor](https://clickport.io/blog/stop-using-google-analytics)).

## Plausible metrics: 18 measures built on a 24-hour visitor hash

Plausible's metric catalog follows from its identity model. Each visitor is `hash(daily_salt + website_domain + ip_address + user_agent)`, and the **salt is rotated and deleted every 24 hours**. Uniqueness therefore holds only within one day, one site and one device ([Plausible: data policy](https://plausible.io/data-policy)). A visit is a run of events from the same User-Agent, IP and domain with gaps under 30 minutes ([Plausible: Events API](https://plausible.io/docs/events-api)). Some consequences are in Plausible's own definitions: the same person counts again on a new day and on each device ([Plausible: metrics definitions](https://plausible.io/docs/metrics-definitions)). Others follow from them. Unique visitors over a multi-day range effectively sum daily uniques. Visitors behind the same office NAT with identical browsers can collapse into one. And no metric that needs cross-day identity can exist, so there are no returning users, cohorts or lifetime value. Those last points are inferences from the hash inputs, not statements by Plausible.

### Plausible collects a short list and refuses the rest

Plausible collects page URLs (query strings stripped except campaign parameters), referrers and UTMs, browser and OS (major/minor version), device type, country, region and city, scroll depth and engagement time, pageviews and custom events, configured props and revenue, timestamps and session identifiers. It does not collect cookies, local storage or cache identifiers, persistent IDs, raw IPs, raw User-Agent strings or exact locations, and it does no cross-device, cross-site or cross-app tracking ([Plausible: data policy](https://plausible.io/data-policy)). The script only reads a `plausible_ignore` flag in local storage, which site owners use to exclude their own visits; it never writes to local storage. Cloud bot filtering stops automation browsers and blocks known bot User-Agents, referrer-spam domains and data-centre IP ranges, and adds an in-house traffic-pattern algorithm. Plausible's own pages disagree on the size of the IP list, **about 32K or about 40,800 ranges**. Community Edition filters only by User-Agent and referrer spam ([Plausible: bot filtering](https://plausible.io/docs/bot-traffic-filtering); [Plausible: self-hosting](https://plausible.io/docs/self-hosting)). The Events API returns 202 even for events it drops, flagged only by an `x-plausible-dropped` header ([Plausible: Events API](https://plausible.io/docs/events-api)).

## Search Console, form leads and consent remain open ground for Rate 1

Rate 1 is cookieless, EU-hosted, targets WordPress, Wix and custom-PHP sites, and adds form leads and a Search Console dashboard. Measured against that, the two product lists sort into three groups. Some items are **parity requirements**: things both incumbents already ship, so Rate 1's absence would be noticed. Some are **structural trade-offs** that come with being cookieless. The rest are **openings** where neither incumbent goes deep.

**Parity.** Rate 1 needs Plausible's metric set as its floor: visitors, visits, pageviews, views per visit, bounce rate, visit duration, current visitors, time on page, scroll depth, exit rate, conversion rate and the conversion counts. It also needs Plausible's report groups: channels including AI Assistants, sources, five UTMs, pages, locations and devices. Period comparison, annotations, saved segments, email reports and spike alerts are table stakes too, because Plausible ships all of them on its $9 tier. Both incumbents added an AI-assistant channel and annotations within the last 20 months, so matching those is now expected rather than distinctive. Bounce rate needs care. The two definitions differ (engaged session versus pageview count), and Plausible changed its own in Nov 2025, so Rate 1 should publish its definition rather than claim parity on the label.

**Trade-offs.** A cookieless product inherits Plausible's constraint. Unless Rate 1 has an identity mechanism Plausible lacks, it can't report returning users, cohorts or lifetime value. If it also uses a daily rotating identifier, its multi-day "unique visitors" will be inflated in the same way. The honest framing is that GA4's richer user metrics come at the price of a banner that, on a small EU site, can remove a large share of visitors outright. Google publishes the 1,000-user modeling threshold, which most small sites never reach ([Google: behavioral modeling](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11161109)). The no-banner claim itself rests on contested ground. EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 treat ePrivacy Art. 5(3) as technology-neutral. Consultation responses warned that their passage on User-Agent and HTTP headers could be read to require consent even for "cookieless" analytics ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/edpb-adopts-guidelines-on-scope-of-eprivacy-directive); [EDPB guidelines v2](https://edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2024-10/edpb_guidelines_202302_technical_scope_art_53_eprivacydirective_v2_en_0.pdf)). Rate 1 can reach parity with Plausible on that question, but it can't claim to be ahead.

**Openings.** Search Console is the clearest gap. GA4 offers no trend charts, can't cross queries with Analytics dimensions, and leaves the collection unpublished by default ([Google: Search Console](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10737381)). Plausible shows only clicked queries, stores nothing, and keeps the data out of its API ([Plausible: Search Console](https://plausible.io/docs/google-search-console-integration)). By inference, a Search Console dashboard that stores the data should beat both: it can offer time series, impression-only queries, query × page joins, and history that builds up past Google's 16-month window. Plausible's note that a 51st connected site disconnects the first also suggests any per-site OAuth design will hit the same Google token limit, which matters for agencies. Form leads are the second gap. GA4 gets lead semantics only after hand-tagging six events. Plausible deliberately counts submissions without content, because its props policy bans names, emails and phone numbers ([Plausible: custom props](https://plausible.io/docs/custom-props/introduction)). Capturing lead content would be a real differentiator, but it also means Rate 1 stores personal data. Its privacy story therefore can't simply copy Plausible's "no personal data" line. It needs a deliberate lead-data posture: a DPA, retention limits and deletion. On platforms, Plausible's official WordPress plugin is a mature benchmark (10,000+ installs, a built-in proxy, stats in WP admin, form-completion goals), while Wix gets only a community guide ([Plausible: integration guides](https://plausible.io/docs/integration-guides)). Rate 1's own WordPress plugin will be compared directly with Plausible's, and an official Wix integration would face no first-party Plausible equivalent.

## Conclusion

A count of metrics would mislead the comparison. GA4's 86-metric and 374-dimension catalog is mostly ad-stack plumbing that a small business never fills. Plausible's 18-metric catalog already covers most of what such a business reads. What separates the products is three things. First, how completely they count: GA4 loses or models declined-consent traffic, while Plausible counts every visit but forgets visitors daily. Second, how deeply they serve the two jobs a small business cares about beyond traffic, which are leads and search visibility. Third, where they put the price gates. Plausible has spent 2025–2026 moving upmarket with funnels, journeys, SSO and consolidated views, all at Business tier or above, so its $9 Starter tier offers no props, funnels or API. That makes room for a product that bundles lead capture and a real Search Console dashboard at an entry-level price.

The window is not permanent. In the last 18 months Google shipped lead-generation reports, Google Business Profile reports, Dashboards and an AI Assistant channel, which shows GA4 moving toward small-business use cases even as its core stays tied to advertising. Its consent dependence, 24–48 hour settling time and 2-month default Explore retention are structural, and they won't change soon. Rate 1's durable advantages are the ones that come from its architecture: complete cookieless counts, EU hosting, and stored first-party Search Console and lead data. Its feature list should be built around those rather than around the length of GA4's metric catalog. Three questions remain open and could change the comparison. The ePrivacy treatment of cookieless analytics is unsettled. Plausible's retention claim is disputed. And neither the GA4 notes nor the Plausible notes cover GA4's WordPress or Wix integration paths.
