# Site Kit closes in on Rate 1's niche

As of November 2026, the four kinds of competitor Rate 1 faces collect very different amounts of data, and the sharpest threat to its WordPress plugin is now Google's free Site Kit rather than Plausible. **Cloudflare Web Analytics** (free, cookieless) reports just **three traffic measures (visits, page views and page load time) across 10 dimensions**, wrapped around a deep Core Web Vitals layer; it counts no unique visitors, sessions, bounces, UTM campaigns, events or goals, keeps only 7 days of unsampled history, and classes its data as US-only. **Plausible** (paid, cookieless, from **$9 a month at 10k pageviews**) exposes **18 metrics and 32 API dimensions** and remains Rate 1's closest architectural peer and its parity floor. **Standard GA4** (free, cookie-based) exposes **86 API metrics, about 80 UI-only metrics and 374 dimensions**, most of them built for ads, ecommerce and apps, and it needs a consent banner in the EU. **GA4 360** (paid, cookie-based) adds **no new metrics**: for an estimated **$50,000 or more a year** it buys 2.5–10× quotas, up to 50 months of retention, subproperties and roll-ups, roughly hourly intraday data and contractual SLAs, none of which a small site needs. **Google Site Kit**, on **5+ million WordPress sites**, spent 2026 shipping Rate 1's own pitch on top of GA4 and Search Console: automatic form-completion tracking for six lead plugins and two shop plugins (Site Goals, generally available 7 Sep 2026), email and PDF reports, and same-day "Recent activity". Its limits mark Rate 1's openings: it shows only Search Console impressions and clicks, with no CTR or position, over at most 90 days; it counts form completions without capturing the lead; and in the EEA, UK and Switzerland it drops every visitor who doesn't consent. No product in any category combines a stored, full-metric Search Console dashboard, lead capture and complete cookieless counting for EU sites, and that combination is where Rate 1 should compete.

The report walks the categories as a grid: free then paid cookieless analytics, then free then paid cookie-based analytics. That puts GA4 360 after standard GA4, so it can be described purely as what it adds. Each product's metrics come before its features. Site Kit follows GA4 directly, as the layer it adds on top of GA4 and Search Console. A side-by-side table across all four categories and the implications for Rate 1 close the report.

## Free and cookieless: Cloudflare counts visits, not visitors

Cloudflare sells two different analytics products, and only one fits a typical small-business site. **Web Analytics** is a JavaScript beacon, which Cloudflare also calls Real User Monitoring (RUM). It needs only a Cloudflare account and a snippet, works without moving DNS or using Cloudflare's proxy, and reads its numbers from the browser's Performance API ([Cloudflare: about](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/about/)). **Manual setup works on any site**: the owner pastes the snippet before `</body>`, only pages that render it are measured, and beacons go to `cloudflareinsights.com`. **Automatic setup works only on proxied zones**: Cloudflare injects the script into every page and subdomain unless Rules limit it, beacons go to the site's own `/cdn-cgi/rum`, and injection fails if the origin sends `Cache-Control: public, no-transform` or invalid HTML ([Cloudflare: FAQ](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/faq/); [Cloudflare: get started](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/get-started/)). The script loads from `static.cloudflareinsights.com/beacon.min.js`, takes timings from Navigation Timing Level 2 (falling back to Level 1), and sends Core Web Vitals when the page is first hidden after load ([Cloudflare: data origin](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/data-metrics/data-origin-and-collection/)). It measures only the initial client request, not Worker subrequests ([Cloudflare: FAQ](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/faq/)).

The second product, **edge zone analytics**, is built from Cloudflare's server logs and exists only for sites behind its proxy. Ad blockers can't block it, because every request is measured, but it carries no site-speed data ([Cloudflare: FAQ](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/faq/); [Cloudflare: web analytics](https://www.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/)). On the **Free plan it shows only requests, bandwidth, an undefined "unique visitors" count and requests by country**, with crawlers and threats included. **Pro, Business and Enterprise** zones add page views (HTML 2xx responses) and visits (referer differs from host), filterable by cache status, data center, source ASN, country, device type, IP, referer host, host, method, HTTP version, path, query string, content type, edge and origin status, security action and source, browser, OS, user agent and X-Requested-With; Enterprise Bot Management adds JA3/JA4 fingerprints. Zone analytics also has Security and Performance tabs, and every plan sees at least 30 days ([Cloudflare: zone analytics](https://developers.cloudflare.com/analytics/account-and-zone-analytics/zone-analytics/)). Cloudflare describes Web Analytics as designed mainly for customers who don't use its proxy ([Cloudflare: FAQ](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/faq/)), and edge analytics requires moving a site's DNS onto Cloudflare, so the rest of this section treats the beacon as the competing product.

### Three traffic measures sit on a deep performance layer

The beacon's traffic layer is thin by design. A **visit** is a page view whose referrer is another website or a direct link, so it is defined by the referrer rather than by a session, and one visit can span several page views. A **page view** is any successful HTTP response with an HTML content type, and single-page-app route changes count too ([Cloudflare: high-level metrics](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/data-metrics/high-level-metrics/)). Cloudflare chose **not to count unique visitors at all**, because doing so would mean storing state about what each visitor does ([Cloudflare blog, Dec 2020](https://blog.cloudflare.com/privacy-first-web-analytics/); [Cloudflare blog: RUM Diaries](https://blog.cloudflare.com/the-rum-diaries-enabling-web-analytics-by-default)). It follows that the product has no sessions, bounce rate, visit duration, entry or exit pages, or returning visitors. By inference from the referrer-based definition, a reload or an internal navigation without a referrer can be miscounted as a visit. The performance layer, by contrast, is deep: a full timing waterfall, the three current Core Web Vitals with sub-part breakdowns added in October 2026, and a Debug View that names the CSS selector of the worst-offending elements.

### Six months of history, unsampled for only the last seven days

Cloudflare gives access to the **previous six months**. Raw beacon data stays unsampled for **7 days**, then is aggregated down to **about 10% of its original volume** for long-term storage. Ingestion itself records every beacon received, but dashboard and API queries are adaptively sampled at anywhere from 0.0001% to 100% depending on filters and row volume; low-traffic sites are sampled at higher rates, and the GraphQL `sampleInterval` field reports the rate used ([Cloudflare: FAQ](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/faq/)). Adaptive Bit Rate picks each chart's resolution from data size and date range ([Cloudflare: about](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/about/)), and Cloudflare's own speed-tool FAQ says RUM is progressively sampled, so some requests are never captured ([Cloudflare: Observatory FAQ](https://developers.cloudflare.com/speed/observatory/faq/)). The default view is the **last 24 hours**; a dropdown changes the range and click-dragging on the graph zooms to a custom period ([Cloudflare: filters](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/configuration-options/filters/)). The research found no documented list of preset ranges and no period-over-period comparison. Older complaints about a 7-day, single-site limit are obsolete ([ctrl.blog](https://www.ctrl.blog/entry/review-cloudflare-analytics/); [Plausible, archived 2021](https://webcf.waybackmachine.org/web/20210423134521/https://plausible.io/vs-cloudflare-web-analytics)). By inference, a small site's monthly and six-month views are built from roughly 10% samples, so its owner sees estimated, rounded numbers for anything older than a week.

### Cookieless and free, but US-held and EU-excluded by default

Cloudflare's privacy design is strict at collection. The beacon uses **no cookies or localStorage** and doesn't fingerprint by IP address or User-Agent ([Cloudflare: Core Web Vitals](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/data-metrics/core-web-vitals/)); Cloudflare says Web Analytics doesn't collect or use visitors' personal data ([Cloudflare: about](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/about/)); edge pre-processing strips IP addresses before data reaches a core data center ([RUM Diaries](https://blog.cloudflare.com/the-rum-diaries-enabling-web-analytics-by-default)); query strings aren't logged ([Cloudflare: FAQ](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/faq/)); and Cloudflare doesn't track individuals across customers' properties or sell the data for ads ([Cloudflare: data origin](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/data-metrics/data-origin-and-collection/); [Cloudflare: web analytics](https://www.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/)). Storage is the weak point. Cloudflare's Data Localization Suite classes the RUM datasets as **US only**, and with an EU metadata boundary their queries return empty results ([Cloudflare: GraphQL datasets](https://developers.cloudflare.com/data-localization/metadata-boundary/graphql-datasets/)). Cloudflare handles GDPR mainly by letting owners drop EU traffic, and makes that the default for auto-enabled Free zones. By inference, an EU small business that accepts the default loses most of its own visitors' data, which is a strong angle for an EU-hosted Rate 1. The 2020 beacon set a `__cfduid` cookie that Cloudflare later retired ([ctrl.blog](https://www.ctrl.blog/entry/review-cloudflare-analytics/)), so "cookieless" holds as of 2026. Plausible, a competitor, adds that the script is closed source and under US jurisdiction ([Plausible: vs Cloudflare](https://plausible.io/vs-cloudflare-web-analytics)).

**Web Analytics is free on every plan, with no traffic cap**; Cloudflare built it to serve sites from about one to a billion requests a day ([Cloudflare blog, Dec 2020](https://blog.cloudflare.com/privacy-first-web-analytics/)). Paid zone plans add only rule slots and the richer edge analytics described above, and Enterprise adds Logpush exports to the customer's own tools ([Cloudflare: limits](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/limits/); [Cloudflare: zone analytics](https://developers.cloudflare.com/analytics/account-and-zone-analytics/zone-analytics/)). Paying more never adds events, UTM or goals to the beacon product.

The documented limitations follow from the design. Adblock Plus, Brave and DuckDuckGo can block the beacon; IE11 sends nothing, and older browsers fall back to XHR, so the last SPA payload can be lost on close ([Cloudflare: FAQ](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/faq/)). CLS is Chromium-only, LCP isn't collected on Safari and Firefox soft navigations, and the 2026 soft-navigation change broke trend comparability for pageviews and visits ([Cloudflare: Core Web Vitals](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/data-metrics/core-web-vitals/); [Cloudflare: soft navigation](https://developers.cloudflare.com/changelog/post/2026-08-21-improved-soft-navigation-measurement-for-single-page-applications/)). Published criticism comes mostly from competitors. Plausible calls the product thin beyond page views, visits, referrers, countries and devices, with no live visitors, bounce rate, visit duration, campaigns or goals ([Plausible: vs Cloudflare](https://plausible.io/vs-cloudflare-web-analytics)); Swetrix says historical reports may count only one visitor in ten ([Swetrix](https://swetrix.com/comparison/cloudflare-analytics/vs-plausible)). An independent 2020 review scored it 6/10, found a server-side bug that logged almost every page view as `/`, noted that reloads re-counted visits, and measured the beacon at **4.31 KB compressed against 19.01 KB for Google Analytics** at the time; whether those bugs persist is unverified ([ctrl.blog](https://www.ctrl.blog/entry/review-cloudflare-analytics/)). No neutral 2025–2026 accuracy benchmark was found.

## Paid and cookieless: Plausible's 18 metrics rest on a 24-hour visitor hash

Plausible's metric catalog follows from its identity model. Each visitor is `hash(daily_salt + website_domain + ip_address + user_agent)`, and the **salt is rotated and deleted every 24 hours**. Uniqueness therefore holds only within one day, one site and one device ([Plausible: data policy](https://plausible.io/data-policy)). A visit is a run of events from the same User-Agent, IP and domain with gaps under 30 minutes ([Plausible: Events API](https://plausible.io/docs/events-api)). Some consequences are in Plausible's own definitions: the same person counts again on a new day and on each device ([Plausible: metrics definitions](https://plausible.io/docs/metrics-definitions)). Others follow from them. Unique visitors over a multi-day range effectively sum daily uniques. Visitors behind the same office NAT with identical browsers can collapse into one. And no metric that needs cross-day identity can exist, so there are no returning users, cohorts or lifetime value. Those last points are inferences from the hash inputs, not statements by Plausible.

### Plausible collects a short list and refuses the rest

Plausible collects page URLs (query strings stripped except campaign parameters), referrers and UTMs, browser and OS (major/minor version), device type, country, region and city, scroll depth and engagement time, pageviews and custom events, configured props and revenue, timestamps and session identifiers. It does not collect cookies, local storage or cache identifiers, persistent IDs, raw IPs, raw User-Agent strings or exact locations, and it does no cross-device, cross-site or cross-app tracking ([Plausible: data policy](https://plausible.io/data-policy)). The script only reads a `plausible_ignore` flag in local storage, which site owners use to exclude their own visits; it never writes to local storage. Cloud bot filtering stops automation browsers and blocks known bot User-Agents, referrer-spam domains and data-centre IP ranges, and adds an in-house traffic-pattern algorithm. Plausible's own pages disagree on the size of the IP list, **about 32K or about 40,800 ranges**. Community Edition filters only by User-Agent and referrer spam ([Plausible: bot filtering](https://plausible.io/docs/bot-traffic-filtering); [Plausible: self-hosting](https://plausible.io/docs/self-hosting)). The Events API returns 202 even for events it drops, flagged only by an `x-plausible-dropped` header ([Plausible: Events API](https://plausible.io/docs/events-api)). The script weighs **about 2.5 KB**, by Plausible's own comparison against Google Analytics ([Plausible: vs Google Analytics](https://plausible.io/vs-google-analytics)).

## Free and cookie-based: GA4's 86 API metrics mostly serve advertisers

GA4 records every interaction as an event: a page view, a scroll and a purchase each arrive as a named event with parameters, and all metrics and dimensions are computed from those events. On a web stream, every event automatically carries `language`, `page_location`, `page_referrer`, `page_title` and `screen_resolution`. Parameter values are capped at **100 characters**, except page_title (300), page_referrer (420) and page_location (1,000) ([Google: automatically collected events](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9234069)). gtag.js also attaches `ga_session_id` (the session start timestamp) and `ga_session_number` to every event in a session. Measurement Protocol and Data Import events don't get them ([Google: sessions](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9191807)). An `is_engaged_session_event` flag on every event feeds engagement rate and bounce rate ([Google: engagement rate](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/12195621)). As of November 2026 the Data API exposes **86 standard metrics and 374 standard dimensions** (schema last updated 10 Nov 2026), and roughly **80 more metrics exist only in the UI and Explorations**. **53 of the 86 API metrics and 195 of the 374 dimensions** serve ecommerce, Google's ad platforms, publisher ads or mobile apps, which leaves a web-traffic core of about 20 metrics ([Google: Data API schema](https://developers.google.com/analytics/devguides/reporting/data/v1/api-schema)).

### A default website gets 4 automatic events and 9 Enhanced Measurement events

With only the tag installed, a GA4 web stream records **`first_visit`, `session_start`, `user_engagement` and `page_view`**. Enhanced Measurement is toggled per web stream and needs no code. It adds outbound clicks, site search, YouTube video, file downloads, form interactions and a scroll event that fires **once, at 90% depth only** ([Google: Enhanced Measurement](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9216061)). Google also makes the site owner responsible for keeping personal data out of these events. Leads, logins, ecommerce and every other business event must be hand-tagged against Google's recommended-event schema. Most of the other "automatic" events exist only in Firebase app streams.

### 86 Data API metrics, of which roughly 20 describe web traffic

The Data API v1 schema lists **86 standard metrics in ten families** ([Google: Data API schema](https://developers.google.com/analytics/devguides/reporting/data/v1/api-schema)). Definitions matter when comparing products. An **engaged session** lasts longer than 10 seconds (adjustable per stream), contains a key event, or has two or more page views. Engagement rate is engaged sessions divided by sessions, and **bounce rate is its complement**. That is a different definition from Universal Analytics' single-hit bounce, so the two numbers aren't comparable ([Google: engagement rate](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/12195621)). Sessions time out after **30 minutes** by default, and the timeout is adjustable ([Google: sessions](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9191807)). GA4 counts users three ways. Total users is anyone who logged an event. Active users, the "Users" shown in reports, are those with an engaged session, a first_visit or recorded engagement time. New users are those with a first_visit ([Google: user metrics](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/12253918)). Reports estimate users and sessions with HyperLogLog++, usually within 1% of the exact counts in a BigQuery export ([Google: sampling](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/13331292)).

### Custom definitions and four layers that alter what reports show

Custom parameters aren't reportable until each one is registered as a custom dimension or metric. Registration isn't retroactive, and new definitions take 24–48 hours to appear. Values that look numeric are coerced to numbers ([Google: custom dimensions and metrics](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10075209)). A standard property allows **25 user-scoped, 50 event-scoped and 10 item-scoped custom dimensions, 50 custom metrics and 5 calculated metrics**, 25 parameters per event and 25 user properties, parameter values of 100 characters, 30 key events and 100 audiences, 50 saved segments and 50 saved comparisons, 200 explorations per user and 500 shared, unlimited distinct event names on web (500 per app user), **200,000 Data API tokens a day**, a **1M-event daily BigQuery export** and 10 GB of data-import storage ([Google: custom dimensions and metrics](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10075209); [Google: Analytics 360 limits](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11202874); [Google: collection limits](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9267744)). User property names can be at most 24 characters and values at most 36, and a User-ID up to 256. The GA4 360 section below sets each of these against its 360 value.

GA4 can alter what a report shows in four ways. **Thresholding** withholds rows when demographics, audiences built on demographics, or low-volume search queries could identify people. It is system-defined and can't be switched off ([Google: thresholds](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9383630)). **Sampling** applies only in Explorations, above **10M events per query** on a standard property ([Google: sampling](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/13331292)). The **"(other)" row** groups the least common values once a table exceeds its row limit. Google treats more than 500 values per dimension as high-cardinality ([Google: other row](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/13331684)). **Modeled data** comes in two forms. Consent-mode behavioral modeling estimates users and sessions, but not event counts, for visitors who decline cookies. It requires advanced consent mode, **at least 1,000 denied-consent events a day for 7 days, and at least 1,000 consenting daily users on 7 of the previous 28 days**. It appears only under the Blended reporting identity and never in BigQuery, audiences, user explorer, cohort, user-lifetime, retention or predictive views ([Google: behavioral modeling](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11161109)). The second form is modeled key events: Google estimates conversions it can't observe, revises attributed conversions for up to 12 days, and credits low-confidence ones to Direct ([Google: modeled key events](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10710245)).

### Two-year cookies, no stored EU IPs, and 2- or 14-month retention

GA4 identifies browsers with two first-party cookies, **`_ga` (client ID) and `_ga_<container-id>` (session state), each with a 2-year default expiry**. The expiry can be overridden to anything from immediate to 25 months. Browsers shorten cookie life for visitors who don't return (Chrome to 400 days, Safari to 7) ([Google: cookie usage](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11397207)). Sites can add a `user_id` of up to 256 characters for signed-in users. It must not contain personal data ([Google: User-ID](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9213390)). When analytics storage is denied through Consent Mode, no client ID is stored ([Google: data collection](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11593727)). Google states that GA4 **does not log or store IP addresses from EU, Swiss or UK users**. Their traffic is collected through EU, Swiss or UK servers, and the IP is used only to derive coarse location before being discarded. **Granular location and device collection** is on by default and can be switched off per region. Switching it off blanks city, browser and OS minor versions, the User-Agent string, device brand, model and name, and screen resolution, and it reduces modeled conversions ([Google: EU data and privacy](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/12017362)). Client-side **data redaction** removes email addresses and any query parameters the owner lists. It is on by default for new properties, covers web streams only, and works on a best-effort basis ([Google: data redaction](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/13544947)). User- and event-level data is kept for **2 or 14 months** on a standard property, and large properties are forced to 2 months. The setting limits only Explorations and funnel reports; aggregated standard reports persist beyond it ([Google: data retention](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/7667196)). Search Console data in GA4 is capped at 16 months, and Google Business Profile data is a rolling 6-month window.

GA4's legal footing in the EU still depends on consent. The 2022 rulings by the Austrian, French and Italian data protection authorities found Google Analytics transfers unlawful ([Matomo GDPR timeline](https://matomo.org/gdpr-timeline/)). The EU General Court upheld the EU-US Data Privacy Framework in *Latombe* on **3 Sep 2025**, but an appeal is pending ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/european-general-court-dismisses-latombe-challenge-upholds-eu-us-data-privacy-framework)). Commentators agree that the Framework settles data transfers, not the ePrivacy cookie-consent requirement ([PrivyCDP](https://privycdp.org/regulation/google-analytics-gdpr-2026)). Small businesses' complaints cluster around the same points. Thresholding can't be disabled; one small WooCommerce store saw about 15% fewer conversions in GA4 than in its store data ([Funnel.io](https://help.funnel.io/en/articles/16746941-troubleshoot-missing-data-caused-by-thresholding-in-ga4)). Data takes 24–48 hours to settle. Explorations keep only 2 months of data by default. And a sub-1,000-user site never reaches the modeling thresholds, so declined consents are simply lost. Vendors selling alternatives put the losses at 30–60% from consent refusal and 10–20% from blockers, but those figures are unverified ([Clickport, vendor](https://clickport.io/blog/stop-using-google-analytics)).

## Free and cookie-based, WordPress layer: Site Kit turns GA4 into a lead dashboard

Google Site Kit is the free, open-source WordPress plugin through which GA4 and Search Console reach most WordPress owners. It has **5+ million active installs** and a **4.2/5 rating from 1,015 reviews, 146 of them one-star**; version 1.192.0 shipped on 16 Nov 2026, and it needs WordPress 5.2+, PHP 7.4+, a publicly reachable site and the REST API ([wordpress.org: Site Kit](https://wordpress.org/plugins/google-site-kit/)). Releases land **exactly 14 days apart**, about 26 a year ([GitHub: Site Kit releases](https://github.com/google/site-kit-wp/releases)). Site Kit stores no analytics of its own. It places the Google tag, sends extra events and custom dimensions into the owner's GA4 property, verifies the site in Search Console, and reads results back through Google's APIs into WordPress admin. What it adds on top of GA4 is therefore four things: pre-built queries with WordPress context (author, category, post date, post type), automatic event tagging for eight plugins, a simpler dashboard with per-page views, and delivery features such as role-based sharing, email reports and PDFs. Its 2026 releases (Site Goals, email and PDF reports, Recent activity, Latest content and AI scaffolding) move it from a GA4 viewer toward an outcome dashboard for small businesses, which is Rate 1's own positioning.

### Search Console stops at impressions, clicks and 90 days

Site Kit's Search Console layer is thinner than GA4's own. The **Search traffic** widget is a four-step funnel: Total Impressions and Total Clicks from Search Console, then Unique Visitors from Search and Conversions (key events) from GA4; clicking a step charts it against the previous period of equal length ([Site Kit: dashboard](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/how-tos/how-to-use-the-site-kit-dashboard/)). Google's development copy of the docs states that **CTR, average position and engagement rate don't appear in this funnel** ([Site Kit dev docs](https://site-kit-dev.appspot.com/documentation/using-site-kit/using-the-site-kit-dashboard/)). The Content tab lists top search queries, with undocumented columns, and a Key Metrics tile, Top performing keywords, shows what people searched for before arriving ([GitHub: key-metrics-widgets.js](https://github.com/google/site-kit-wp/blob/develop/assets/js/components/KeyMetrics/key-metrics-widgets.js)). Ranges are 7, 14, 28 (default) or **90 days at most** ([Site Kit: dashboard](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/how-tos/how-to-use-the-site-kit-dashboard/)), against 16 months in GA4's own Search Console reports ([Google: Search Console](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10737381)). Per-page figures come from URL Search, which filters the whole dashboard to one page, and from the admin bar, which shows impressions and clicks for the current URL over 28 days ([Site Kit: page-specific metrics](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/features/page-specific-metrics/)); the WordPress dashboard widget shows site-wide totals ([Site Kit: dashboard sharing](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/features/dashboard-sharing/)). New connections show a gathering-data message for up to about 48 hours ([Site Kit: Search Console](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/supported-services/search-console/)). The **Recent activity** tab (1.190.0, 19 Oct 2026) adds traffic and search performance since yesterday, with up to 6 hours of lag ([GitHub: releases](https://github.com/google/site-kit-wp/releases); [Site Kit dev docs](https://site-kit-dev.appspot.com/documentation/using-site-kit/using-the-site-kit-dashboard/)). Since January 2026, Yoast SEO's dashboard can also display Search Console and GA data through Site Kit, though that was seen only in a search result ([Yoast, 7 Jan 2026](https://yoast.com/yoast-seo-january-7-2026/)). ### Site Goals counts form completions from eight plugins, without the lead

Site Kit's answer to lead tracking is **Plugin conversion tracking**, formerly called enhanced conversion tracking. It detects eight active plugins (**Contact Form 7, MailChimp, OptinMonster, Popup Maker, WPForms, WooCommerce, Ninja Forms and Easy Digital Downloads**) and adds front-end JavaScript that sends standard GA4 events to GA4, Google Ads or both; one toggle serves Ads and Analytics ([Site Kit: plugin conversion tracking](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/features/plugin-conversion-tracking/)). Commerce plugins send `purchase` and `add_to_cart`; lead plugins send `submit_lead_form`, `contact` and `generate_lead`. Google's docs say the tracking is typically on by default, and they assign WooCommerce and Easy Digital Downloads to online stores and Contact Form 7, MailChimp for WooCommerce, Ninja Forms, OptinMonster, Popup Maker and WPForms to lead generation ([Site Kit: Site goals](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/features/site-goals/)). PPC Land distinguishes this from Google Ads Enhanced Conversions, which send hashed emails and similar data ([PPC Land, Oct 2026](https://ppc.land/google-site-kit-auto-tracks-checkouts-and-form-leads-from-8-wordpress-plugins/)).

**Site Goals** turns those events into two dashboard widgets of up to six tiles each. **Online store performance** shows Sales Rate and Total Sales (falling back to Add to Cart Rate and products added), Engagement Rate, a Products added to cart tile, goal-driver tiles, and Other sources for unsupported plugins that fire the same events. **Lead generation performance** shows form completion rate, total form completions, Engagement Rate, goal-driver tiles and Other form completions. Tile options include top traffic channels, sales or leads by visitor type, top pages, top authors, locations and device type. An **Advanced data breakdown** splits results by plugin or by individual form by creating the GA4 custom dimensions `googlesitekit_event_provider` and `googlesitekit_form_id`; it isn't backfilled and needs the GA4 Editor or Administrator role ([Site Kit: Site goals](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/features/site-goals/); [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/google-site-kit-auto-tracks-checkouts-and-form-leads-from-8-wordpress-plugins/)). Site Goals shipped behind a flag in 1.179.0 (18 May 2026), became **generally available in 1.187.0 on 7 Sep 2026**, and was promoted by Google Search Central on 15 Sep 2026 ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/google-site-kit-auto-tracks-checkouts-and-form-leads-from-8-wordpress-plugins/); [GitHub: releases](https://github.com/google/site-kit-wp/releases)).

Site Kit also began generating its own **content events** in 2026: a content-events provider for all sites (1.186.0, 24 Aug), outbound-link and contact-link tracking (1.188.0, 21 Sep), an event when a reader finishes a post (1.189.0, 5 Oct), and a prompt to re-enable plugin conversion tracking plus a Site Goals insight data builder (1.192.0, 16 Nov) ([GitHub: releases](https://github.com/google/site-kit-wp/releases)). ### Consent, lag and setup failures limit what Site Kit shows

Site Kit puts the standard Google tag on the page for GA4 and Ads, so GA4's two-year `_ga` cookies apply ([Site Kit: Analytics](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/supported-services/analytics/); [Google: cookie usage](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11397207)). Google leaves notice and consent to the site owner ([wordpress.org: Site Kit](https://wordpress.org/plugins/google-site-kit/)) and lists CookieYes, Complianz, GDPR Cookie Compliance (with a premium add-on) and Cookiebot as compatible banners ([Site Kit: GDPR and privacy](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/advanced/gdpr-compliance-and-privacy/)). With consent mode on and no consent-management platform, **EEA, UK and Swiss visitors are excluded from Analytics and Ads reporting**, and visitors who decline are excluded too ([Site Kit: consent mode](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/features/consent-mode/)). Three inferences follow. The reference to modeling implies advanced consent mode, though the docs never name the variant; visitors outside the EEA, UK and Switzerland are tracked with cookies by default; and because GA4's behavioral modeling needs at least 1,000 consenting users a day ([Google: behavioral modeling](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11161109)), most small EU sites lose the excluded visitors outright. The plugin FAQ's claim that IP anonymization is on by default is a Universal Analytics leftover, since GA4 doesn't store EU IPs at all ([Google: EU data and privacy](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/12017362)). The tag gateway makes collection more resilient to blockers; it doesn't reduce what reaches Google ([Site Kit: tag gateway](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/features/google-tag-gateway-for-advertisers/)).

The documented limitations are long. The site must be public, so password-protected homepages and maintenance or coming-soon plugins break setup; the REST API must be reachable; ad blockers break AdSense requests; caching plugins (WP Super Cache, Breeze, Autoptimize) and relative-URL plugins interfere with setup; WPML and TranslatePress can produce "no data", and WPML multi-domain is unsupported; there is **no multisite network support and no headless support**; and some regions block Google services ([Site Kit: known limitations](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/troubleshooting/known-limitations/)). Data arrives slowly and without history: up to 48 hours of gathering on connection, up to 6 hours of lag on Recent activity, PDFs that exclude today, and Visitor groups and Site Goals breakdowns that take 24–48 hours and are never backfilled ([Site Kit: Visitor groups](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/features/visitor-groups/); [Site Kit: Site goals](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/features/site-goals/); [Site Kit: PDF reports](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/features/pdf-reports/)). Customization is capped at 8 Key Metrics tiles and 6 tiles per Site Goals widget, with a fixed email layout ([Site Kit: Key metrics](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/features/key-metrics/); [Site Kit: email reports](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/features/email-reports/)). Permissions add friction: insufficient-permission notices appear when Google access changes, and Visitor groups and Site Goals breakdowns need extra OAuth grants and the GA4 Editor role ([Site Kit: Search Console](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/supported-services/search-console/); [Site Kit: Visitor groups](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/features/visitor-groups/)). The six custom dimensions Site Kit creates also count, by inference, against the property's custom-dimension quota. One-star review titles cluster around setup loops and failures, site slowdowns (including one reviewer slowed by Google Ads code they didn't use) and outright breakage such as HTTP 500 errors ([wordpress.org: 1-star reviews](https://wordpress.org/support/plugin/google-site-kit/reviews/?filter=1)); how often each complaint occurs is unknown.

## Paid and cookie-based: GA4 360 buys scale and SLAs, not new metrics

Google Analytics 360 is the same product as standard GA4 under a contract. It uses the same tag, events, metrics, dimensions, cookies and consent requirements, and Google's own standard-versus-360 comparison lists differences only in limits, service levels, property structure and billing ([Google: Analytics 360](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11202874)). What a 360 customer buys falls into six groups: higher quotas, multi-property structures, fuller data access, faster processing, contractual SLAs, and enterprise administration and support. Several UA-era selling points no longer separate the tiers. Standard GA4 reports aren't sampled, since sampling applies only in Explorations ([Google: sampling](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/13331292)); streaming BigQuery export is open to everyone; and the Search Ads 360, Display & Video 360 and Campaign Manager 360 links work on standard properties ([Louder: SA360](https://louder.com.au/2022/03/30/search-ads-360-and-google-analytics-4-integration); [Louder: DV360](https://louder.com.au/2022/03/08/dv360-and-ga4-integration); [Google: Floodlight](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/14615126)). Google's 2026 release notes, from 16 Jan to 6 Nov, contain **no 360-specific items**, and the last one was GA360 property syncing on 2 Jun 2025 ([Google: what's new 2026](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9164320); [Google: what's new archive](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/13420269)).

### SLAs promise 99.9% collection, with credits capped at 25%

The SLAs are the clearest contractual difference, and they are narrower than the marketing suggests. The current SLA document is dated 15 Dec 2022 ([Google: 360 SLA](https://marketingplatform.google.com/about/analytics_products/sla/)).

| SLA | Target | Credit if missed |
|---|---|---|
| Collection | 99.9% uptime | 5% (96 to under 99.9%), 10% (93–96%), 15% (90–93%), 25% (under 90%) |
| Reporting | 99% uptime of UI login and reporting requests; not for XL properties | Same bands, from under 99% |
| Data processing | 98% of the time: Normal properties within **4 h of receipt**, Large within 48 h of midnight PT, XL within 7 days | Same bands, from under 98% |

Source: [360 SLA](https://marketingplatform.google.com/about/analytics_products/sla/).

Credits are capped at **25% of the month's 360 fees**; customers may terminate if SLAs are missed three months running or four times in twelve, and claims must reach Google support within 30 days. Integration features (Google Ads, AdSense, BigQuery, Firebase), beta and alpha features, UA properties and customer-initiated upgrades are excluded, and coverage starts only once the account is on a 360 contract for GA4 ([Google: 360 SLA](https://marketingplatform.google.com/about/analytics_products/sla/); [Google: Analytics 360](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11202874)). Realtime (under 1 min), unsampled explorations (1 h), attribution (4–8 h), user lifetime, Insights and expanded data sets (24 h each), daily BigQuery export (24 h after midnight) and Data Import (24–48 h) run outside the processing SLA ([Google: non-standard processing](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10742670)), and Realtime, Data API and Audience List API queries fall outside the Reporting SLA ([Google: reporting SLA exceptions](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10999787)). A BigQuery export SLA nominally covers Fresh Daily but is described as not currently available, which conflicts with Google's product page claim that SLAs run through BigQuery daily export ([Google: BigQuery export SLA](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/14117050); [Google: 360 product page](https://marketingplatform.google.com/about/analytics-360/)).

### No public price; estimates start near $50,000 a year

Google publishes **no price list**. 360 is sold by quote through Google sales or Marketing Platform Partners, on **annual contracts billed by event volume** ([Google: 360 product page](https://marketingplatform.google.com/about/analytics-360/); [Google: Analytics 360](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11202874)). Upgrading a property changes nothing on the bill until the account moves onto a 360 contract. Events copied into a subproperty or roll-up are processed again and bill at **an extra 0.5 of an event** each ([Google: account structure](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9679158)); one third-party example bills 100 events copied to one subproperty as 150 ([userinsight.ai](https://userinsight.ai/google-analytics-360-pricing)). InfoTrust, a reseller, says licenses are annual, start on the first of a month and come with no short-term option ([InfoTrust, Feb 2025](https://infotrust.com/articles/ga360-insights-and-recommendations/)); Google's 2017 Americas reseller terms auto-renew yearly unless notice comes 30 days before expiry ([Google: customer minimum terms](https://marketingplatform.google.com/about/analytics/reseller-cmt/na/)). Every dollar figure is a third-party estimate. Rybbit's Sep 2026 roundup reports Cardinal Path's 2022 retail start of **$50,000 a year for 25M events a month**, Vision Labs' calculator of **$4,200 a month for 25M events plus $17.50 per extra million**, and a typical **$50,000–$150,000+** band ([Rybbit](https://rybbit.com/blog/google-analytics-pricing)); Embertribe ties the top of that band to about 500M monthly hits ([Embertribe](https://embertribe.com/blog/google-analytics-cost)), and OptimizeSmart expects $150K or more ([OptimizeSmart](https://optimizesmart.com/blog/ga4-vs-ga4-360-pricing-limits-billing-and-more/)). The widely quoted $150K figure traces back to Universal Analytics 360 in 2020 ([Rybbit](https://rybbit.com/blog/google-analytics-pricing)). Applying the Vision Labs formula gives roughly $50K a year at 25M monthly events, $66K at 100M, $150K at 500M and $255K at 1B. That calculation is this report's, not a quote. Fresh Daily and streaming exports add GCP charges, and implementation is usually a separate agency fee ([Google: Fresh Daily](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/15604391); [Rybbit](https://rybbit.com/blog/google-analytics-pricing)).

### Same cookies and consent, with longer retention and contractual privacy terms

Nothing in 360's documentation changes how data is collected, so GA4's banner requirement, consent-driven data loss and US-headquartered processing apply unchanged. The privacy differences are contractual. EEA, UK and Swiss 360 customers have Google's data processing terms built into their agreements, and partner-bought customers need DP terms with Google or the partner ([Google: data processing terms](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/3379636)). Retention can stretch to 50 months, subproperties can restrict who sees which data, and a deletion request on a source property also removes the data from its roll-ups ([Google: Analytics 360](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11202874); [Google: roll-ups](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11526039)). Google pitches 360 at large enterprises, with a retailer as its showcase customer ([Google: 360 product page](https://marketingplatform.google.com/about/analytics-360/)); Embertribe judges free GA4 sufficient below about 500M monthly events ([Embertribe](https://embertribe.com/blog/google-analytics-cost)). A small business site uses a fraction of standard GA4's 10M-event Explore threshold, 1M-event daily export and 200,000-token API quota, so for Rate 1's market 360 is the ceiling of the cookie-based category rather than a real option.

**Parity.** Plausible's metric set remains Rate 1's floor: visitors, visits, pageviews, views per visit, bounce rate, visit duration, current visitors, time on page, scroll depth, exit rate, conversion rate and the conversion counts, with channels including AI assistants, sources, five UTMs, pages, locations and devices. Period comparison, annotations, saved segments, email reports and spike alerts are table stakes too, because Plausible ships all of them on its $9 tier. Site Kit now sets a second floor specific to WordPress, because a site owner will compare Rate 1's plugin with the Google plugin already in their admin menu. That floor includes per-page stats in the admin bar, a WordPress dashboard widget, a URL filter for the whole dashboard, view-only sharing to WordPress roles that need no outside account, scheduled email reports and PDF export, breakdowns by author, category and post type, a new-versus-returning split, and lead counts by channel, page, city, device and author ([Site Kit: dashboard sharing](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/features/dashboard-sharing/); [GitHub: key-metrics-widgets.js](https://github.com/google/site-kit-wp/blob/develop/assets/js/components/KeyMetrics/key-metrics-widgets.js)). Cloudflare adds no traffic-metric parity requirement, since it reports less than Plausible. Its free Web Vitals, alongside Site Kit's PageSpeed tab, mean a WordPress owner already sees site speed at no cost; Plausible omits speed entirely, so a speed panel is a product choice for Rate 1 rather than an obligation. Bounce rate needs care: GA4 (and Site Kit, which reuses its engagement rate) defines it as a session that wasn't engaged, Plausible counts pageviews and interactive events, and Plausible changed its own definition in Nov 2025, so Rate 1 should publish its definition rather than claim parity on the label.

**Trade-offs.** A cookieless product inherits Plausible's constraint. Unless Rate 1 has an identity mechanism Plausible lacks, it can't report returning users, cohorts or lifetime value, and if it uses a daily rotating identifier its multi-day unique visitors will be inflated in the same way. Site Kit's new and returning visitor groups exist only because it rides GA4's cookie, while Cloudflare shows the opposite extreme: by refusing any state, it can't count unique visitors at all. The honest framing is that GA4's richer user metrics come at the price of a banner that, on a small EU site, removes a large share of visitors outright, because Google's modeling needs at least 1,000 consenting users a day ([Google: behavioral modeling](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/11161109)). The no-banner claim itself rests on contested ground. EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 treat ePrivacy Art. 5(3) as technology-neutral, and consultation responses warned that their passage on User-Agent and HTTP headers could be read to require consent even for cookieless analytics ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/edpb-adopts-guidelines-on-scope-of-eprivacy-directive); [EDPB guidelines v2](https://edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2024-10/edpb_guidelines_202302_technical_scope_art_53_eprivacydirective_v2_en_0.pdf)). Rate 1 can match Plausible and Cloudflare on that question, but it can't claim to be ahead.

**Openings.** Search Console remains the clearest gap, and it is now a four-way gap. Cloudflare has no integration; Plausible shows only clicked queries, stores nothing and keeps the data out of its API ([Plausible: Search Console](https://plausible.io/docs/google-search-console-integration)); GA4 offers no trend charts, can't cross queries with Analytics dimensions and leaves the collection unpublished by default ([Google: Search Console](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/10737381)); and Site Kit shows impressions and clicks without CTR or position, over 90 days at most ([Site Kit dev docs](https://site-kit-dev.appspot.com/documentation/using-site-kit/using-the-site-kit-dashboard/)). By inference, a Search Console dashboard that stores the data beats all four: it can offer CTR and position trends, impression-only queries, query × page joins, and history that builds up beyond Google's 16-month window. Plausible's note that a 51st connected site disconnects the first suggests any per-site OAuth design will hit the same Google token limit, which matters for agencies.

Form leads are still open, but the gap narrowed on 7 Sep 2026. Site Kit now gives WordPress owners free completion counts by channel, page, city, device and author for six lead plugins, so "we count your form submissions" is no longer a differentiator on WordPress. What remains distinctive is everything Site Kit's design rules out. It tracks only Contact Form 7, MailChimp, Ninja Forms, OptinMonster, Popup Maker and WPForms, so by inference Gravity Forms, Fluent Forms, Formidable, Elementor, Jetpack and HubSpot forms go uncounted unless they emit GA4 event names. It has no Wix or PHP path. Its per-form breakdown needs new GA4 custom dimensions and the Editor role. And it drops non-consenting EU visitors ([Site Kit: Site goals](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/features/site-goals/); [Site Kit: consent mode](https://sitekit.withgoogle.com/documentation/features/consent-mode/)). Neither Google nor Plausible captures the lead itself: Site Kit reports counts and rates, and Plausible's props policy bans names, emails and phone numbers ([Plausible: custom props](https://plausible.io/docs/custom-props/introduction)). Capturing lead content would be a real differentiator, but it means Rate 1 stores personal data, so its privacy story can't copy Plausible's no-personal-data line. It needs a deliberate lead-data posture: a DPA, retention limits and deletion.

Completeness for EU sites is the third opening, and the two free products sharpen it. Cloudflare's auto-enabled Free zones exclude EU visitors by default and keep RUM data US-only ([Cloudflare blog: RUM Diaries](https://blog.cloudflare.com/the-rum-diaries-enabling-web-analytics-by-default); [Cloudflare: GraphQL datasets](https://developers.cloudflare.com/data-localization/metadata-boundary/graphql-datasets/)). Site Kit excludes every EEA, UK and Swiss visitor who doesn't consent. An EU small business using either free option therefore undercounts its own home audience, which an EU-hosted cookieless product counts in full. On platforms, Plausible's official WordPress plugin (10,000+ installs, built-in proxy, WP-admin stats, form-completion goals) is the cookieless benchmark, Site Kit (5M+ installs) is the incumbent in WP admin, and Wix gets only a community guide from Plausible and a manual snippet from Cloudflare ([Plausible: integration guides](https://plausible.io/docs/integration-guides)). An official Wix integration would face no first-party equivalent from either cookieless rival.

**Threats.** Site Kit is the competitor moving fastest toward Rate 1. It ships every 14 days, and its 2026 releases added Site Goals, email and PDF reports, Recent activity, Latest content and outbound, contact-link and read-to-end events; release 1.192.0 added AI disclosure components alongside a Site Goals insight builder ([GitHub: releases](https://github.com/google/site-kit-wp/releases)), which points, by inference, to AI-written insights in an early-2027 release. Cloudflare's default-on rollout puts free, cookieless analytics in front of every free proxied site without an install ([Cloudflare blog: RUM Diaries](https://blog.cloudflare.com/the-rum-diaries-enabling-web-analytics-by-default)), though its EU exclusion and missing events limit the threat for EU lead-generation sites. GA4 360 is no threat in Rate 1's market. Its 50-month retention gives Rate 1 a talking point instead, because long retention sits in tension with GDPR's storage-limitation principle; that is an inference, not a regulator's finding.

## Conclusion

The cookie line no longer predicts who competes with Rate 1. Plausible is the closest match in architecture and Cloudflare in price, but the product converging on Rate 1's actual pitch (form leads, search visibility and plain-language reports inside WordPress) is Google's free plugin, which in 2026 started tagging leads for owners and emailing them reports. Site Kit can't follow Rate 1 everywhere, though. It can't count the EU visitors GA4's consent model excludes, can't store Search Console data it only reads through Google's APIs, can't capture lead content GA4's policies keep out, and can't leave WordPress. Cloudflare, for its part, has stayed a performance tool with a thin traffic counter for six years, and it handles EU privacy by dropping EU visitors. Rate 1's durable advantages are therefore architectural: complete cookieless counts on EU infrastructure, stored first-party Search Console history with CTR and position, lead capture across more form tools and platforms than eight WordPress plugins, and first-party Wix and PHP support. Its roadmap should be measured against Site Kit's two-week release cadence, not against the length of GA4's metric catalog.

Several questions remain open and could shift the comparison. The ePrivacy treatment of cookieless analytics is unsettled. Plausible's unlimited-retention claim is disputed. Site Kit's top-queries columns and per-plugin event map are undocumented. Cloudflare's 2020 counting bugs haven't been rechecked. GA4's own Wix and PHP integration paths weren't covered by this research. And Site Kit's AI insights, once they ship, may change how much of its value depends on a site owner reading dashboards at all.
